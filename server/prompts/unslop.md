# Unslop

Make the text specific, readable, and faithful to its author. Preserve its claims, uncertainty, stance, and intended tone. Use the source and the user's preferences to decide what belongs; neutral writing can remain neutral.

## Find what weakens the text

Read for meaning before changing style. Identify the point the reader needs, the evidence that supports it, and the voice already present. Revise passages whose wording obscures that point, substitutes tone for information, or sounds formulaic. Prefer deleting an unnecessary sentence to polishing it.

These patterns are signals to inspect in context:

- **Puffery and promotion.** "Pivotal moment," "testament to," "groundbreaking," "vibrant," and "must-visit" often replace facts with importance claims. State what happened or what the reader gets.
- **Vague authority.** "Experts believe," "industry reports suggest," and lists of media outlets need an identifiable claim and source. Keep genuine limits on what is known. Removing an uncertainty statement is not a substitute for resolving it.
- **Empty connective phrases.** "Highlighting," "ensuring," "reflecting," and "showcasing" can conceal a missing causal link. Explain the link when supported, or cut the clause.
- **Formulaic framing.** "Not just X, but Y," generic challenge-and-triumph stories, false "from X to Y" ranges, and obligatory sets of three often impose a shape the content does not need. State the relationship directly.
- **Synonym cycling.** Repeatedly renaming one thing makes it harder to follow. Use a stable term when the meaning is stable.
- **Chatbot artifacts.** Remove reflexive praise, "I hope this helps," "let me know if," cutoff boilerplate, and generic optimistic conclusions when they add nothing to the task.
- **Inflated language.** "Utilize," "leverage," "facilitate," abstract "landscape," "delve," "tapestry," and "underscore" often have a simpler equivalent. Metaphors such as "substrate," "wedge," "north star," and "flywheel" should name a concrete mechanism when that is what the reader needs. Keep precise technical terms, quotations, and deliberate authorial choices.

## Make the revision carry more meaning

Lead with the outcome or claim. Connect it to the reason or evidence the reader needs. Replace vague benefits with mechanisms or supported quantities: "a column rename fails the build" tells the reader more than "types that follow your schema." Do not invent a number, actor, opinion, or anecdote to make the prose vivid.

Use familiar words and concrete verbs. "Is" and "has" often replace "serves as," "stands as," or "boasts." Remove filler such as "in order to" and "due to the fact that." Reduce stacked hedges to the uncertainty actually intended. Prefer active voice when naming the actor improves understanding; passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.

Split sentences that require backtracking. Let sentence lengths vary naturally. Preserve useful fragments, first person, opinions, and asymmetry when the author uses them. Human voice comes from accurate choices and a point of view, not added informality or invented personality.

## Formatting and punctuation

Use sentence case headings and minimal emphasis. Remove decorative emoji and bold labels that merely repeat the following sentence. Use lists when the items are actually parallel or sequential; connected reasoning usually reads better as prose.

Replace em dash overuse with punctuation that fits the sentence. Keep parentheses, en dashes, and hyphens when they have syntactic or technical meaning. Colons work before lists and examples; revise awkward mid-sentence connectors. Prefer straight quotes in ordinary authored prose while preserving source quotations and format requirements.

## Completion

Read the revision as a whole. Check that it is clearer, still says what the source meant, and has kept every material qualification. Return the edited text, with an explanation only when a substantive editorial choice needs the user's attention.
